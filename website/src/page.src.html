NAFO 4VsWEastern Scotian Shelf cod · model briefing for fisheries managers

Would fishing forage fish bring back eastern Scotian Shelf cod?

Cod on the eastern Scotian Shelf collapsed in the early 1990s and did not come back after the {{moratorium_year}} moratorium. Herring, sand lance and capelin increased at the same time. One explanation is that predator and prey swapped roles: forage fish, freed from cod, now eat and outcompete young cod. If that is right, fishing forage fish could release cod. We tested the idea with a size-based model of the shelf's fish community.

The short answer

  • On present evidence, fishing forage fish is not a supported way to rebuild this cod stock.
  • Driven by the real fishing history, the model's cod is back above half its 1980s level {{hindA_years_to_50}} years after the stock's {{hindA_year_min}} low. Fishing alone does not explain why the real stock stayed down.
  • Raising the death rate of adult cod to the level estimated after the collapse, which includes grey seal predation, reproduces the decline. While that death rate stays high, none of the forage-fishing programmes tested brings cod back.
  • Forage fishing restores cod only from a cod-free state that exists under one untested assumption and that the model never reaches from the real history. From the model's own depleted 2025 state it delays recovery by {{dep_delay_min}} to {{dep_delay_max}} years.
  • The costs are less food for cod, seabirds and seals, and forage stocks fished down to a small fraction of their size.

These are model results. They are no forecast and no recommendation; use the years shown to compare options with each other.

Observations

What happened

Cod landings peaked at {{landings_peak_kt:.0f}} thousand tonnes (kt) in {{landings_peak_year}} and averaged {{landings_1980s_mean_kt:.0f}} kt a year through the 1980s. The spawning stock, estimated from the summer research survey, peaked at {{ssb_peak_kt:.0f}} kt in {{ssb_peak_year}} and was down to {{ssb_2002_kt:.1f}} kt by 2002, a fall of {{ssb_decline_frac:.0%}}. The directed fishery closed in September {{moratorium_year}}. Since then only bycatch has been landed: {{landings_1994_t:int}} t in 1994 and {{landings_2002_t:int}} t in 2002. The survey's cod biomass index was {{rv_index_2023_t:int}} t in 2023 and {{rv_index_2024_t:int}} t in 2024.

Over the same years the survey caught many more forage fish. Averaged over 1995–2004, the survey index for herring, sand lance and capelin together was {{survey_forage_1995_2004:x}} its 1980s average, while cod was at {{survey_cod_1995_2004:x}}. Haddock, silver hake and American plaice fell too, to between {{survey_plaice_1995_2004:x}} and {{survey_haddock_1995_2004:x}}. The forage fish declined again to {{survey_forage_2005_12:x}} in 2005–12 without cod returning. Grey seals on Sable Island multiplied throughout.

Read the survey numbers with care

The summer survey is a bottom trawl. It samples schooling herring and buried sand lance poorly, so single years are noisy. In the 1980s sand lance and capelin were scarce in the survey, which inflates their later ratios. The series shown here are derived from a public compilation of DFO survey data and are indicative; official series should replace them before any formal use.

The hypothesis

The idea tested: predator and prey swap roles

Adult cod eat forage fish. Forage fish eat cod eggs and larvae and compete with young cod for plankton. While cod are plentiful they keep forage fish down, which protects their own young. Once cod are scarce, forage fish multiply and may hold each new generation of cod down.

If this loop is strong enough, the shelf can settle into a second, cod-poor state that lasts even with no cod fishing. Fishing forage fish hard could then break the loop and let cod back in.

Grey seals offer a competing explanation: more seals, and other causes, raised the death rate of adult cod. Estimates of adult natural mortality for 1990–2004 average about {{adult_M_1990_2004}} per year, meaning roughly {{adult_M_frac_high:.0%}} of adult cod die each year from causes other than fishing, against about {{adult_M_frac_normal:.0%}} at the {{adult_M_normal}} usually assumed for a healthy stock.

The untested assumption (s = {{s_value}})

In the model, forage fish eat cod eggs, larvae and small juveniles {{s_value}} times more readily than plankton of the same size. With this setting they cause {{forage_share_cod_deaths:.1%}} of deaths of young cod between 1 mg and 1 g at 1980s abundance.

No study on this shelf has found forage fish eating cod eggs or larvae. In the North Sea, herring ate at most {{north_sea_herring_egg_max_pct}}% of cod eggs. A direct test on this shelf found cod recruitment unrelated to forage-fish biomass. Every result below that depends on the role reversal depends on this setting.

Model compared with the record

What the model found with the real fishing history

The model follows seven species (cod, haddock, silver hake, American plaice, herring, sand lance and capelin) and the plankton they eat, by body size. Its 1980–85 state matches the survey biomasses to within {{max_biomass_error:.1%}} and gives cod landings of {{model_cod_yield_kt:.0f}} kt against {{observed_landings_kt}} kt reported. It was then driven by the documented fishing pressure from 1970 to 2025.

Fishing only

Cod falls to {{hindA_ssb_1993_rel:pct}} of its 1980s spawning stock in 1993, close to the survey, then recovers: it is back above half the 1980s level by {{hindA_year_50}}. Over 1995–2004 the model has cod at {{hindA_cod_ratio:x}} its 1980s level, where the survey shows {{survey_cod_1995_2004:x}}. Forage fish rise only to {{hindA_forage_ratio:x}}, where the survey shows {{survey_forage_1995_2004:x}}.

Fishing plus higher adult cod mortality

Adult cod natural mortality is raised to {{adult_M_1990_2004}} per year from 1990, the estimate after the collapse. Cod over 1995–2004 is then at {{hindB_cod_ratio:x}}, close to the survey's {{survey_cod_1995_2004:x}}, and at {{hindB_ssb_2025_rel:pct}} of its 1980s spawning stock in 2025. Forage fish still rise only to {{hindB_forage_ratio:x}}. With the forage-fish effect switched off (s = 1), cod in 2000–02 is {{hindB_mech_effect:.0%}} higher: the adult death rate does almost all the work.

What this means. In this model, fishing alone does not explain why cod stayed down, at any strength of the assumption tested (see What it would take). A higher adult death rate does. The model also cannot make forage fish boom the way the survey shows, so it cannot test the role reversal at the size of boom that actually happened.

Sensitivity

What it would take for forage fish to hold cod down

Two things decide whether forage fish can trap cod in this model: how much they prey on young cod (the assumption), and how far the community has already tipped, meaning how large the forage boom is and how few cod are left. Each assumption strength below was recalibrated so the 1980s state still matches the survey. It is shown as the share of deaths of young cod (1 mg to 1 g) caused by forage fish at 1980s abundance.

Up to {{map_share_s10:pct}} of young-cod deaths, cod comes back on its own from every start in the grid, however large the boom and however few cod are left. In the main model ({{map_share_s20:pct}}), cod is held down in {{map_stuck_s20}} of {{map_cells_per_panel}} starts: with the survey's boom ({{survey_forage_1995_2004:x}}) only when cod starts at 2% of its 1980s level or less, and with a boom twice that size also at 4%. With the survey's boom and cod at the survey's own post-collapse levels, cod is back to half its 1980s level in {{map_s20_cod04_boom787_years}} years from 4% and {{map_s20_cod10_boom787_years}} years from 10%.

At {{map_share_s40:pct}} of young-cod deaths cod is held down in {{map_stuck_s40}} of {{map_cells_per_panel}} starts, including the survey's own cod levels. At that setting, though, the model's 1980s state is itself unstable, so these long-run outcomes are not reliable. Driven by the real fishing history, this setting still has cod back above half its 1980s level within {{sens_max_years}} years of the 1993 low.

Implications

What this means for decisions

On present evidence, fishing forage fish is not a supported lever for rebuilding eastern Scotian Shelf cod.

Why

The model brings cod back after the moratorium unless adult cod die at the elevated rate estimated after the collapse. With that rate held, none of the {{all_M_n_runs}} explorer runs with high adult deaths brings cod back, from any starting state and with or without forage fishing. From the model's own 2025 state, forage fishing takes {{dep_prog_min_years}} to {{dep_prog_max_years}} years to reach half the 1980s spawning stock, against {{dep_sq_years}} years without it, because cod lose food.

The narrow case where it could work

If forage fish really do eat young cod at the assumed rate, and the shelf has reached the cod-free state with forage fish at about {{alt_forage_cod_free:.0f}} times their 1980s level, a short, hard forage fishery restores cod in the model: at least F {{alt_min_F_D5}} for 5 years, F {{alt_min_F_D10}} for 10 years, or F {{alt_min_F_D15}} for 15 years or longer. Cod then needs {{alt_rec_years_min}} to {{alt_rec_years_max}} years to reach half its 1980s level. The model's history never reaches that state, and adult cod deaths must also have returned to normal.

What forage fishing would cost

Food for cod

On the banks, large cod eat mainly sand lance. In the model's long-run states, cod at age 5 weigh {{eq_cod_w5_sq_kg:.2f}} kg with today's forage fishing and {{eq_cod_w5_F1_kg:.2f}} kg when forage fish are fished at F 1.

Seabirds

Across the world's seabird colonies, breeding success drops once prey falls below about a third of its highest level. Seabirds are not in the model, so this cost is not counted in any run.

Seals and other mammals

Grey seals near Sable Island ate mostly sand lance ({{seal_diet_sand_lance:.0%}} of their diet by weight in 1991–93). Fewer sand lance could push seals onto cod. Seals are not a species in the model, so this feedback is missing.

The forage stocks themselves

From the model's 2025 state, forage F of 0.5 or more for 10 years or longer takes forage fish down to {{dep_hardF_forage_low_max:pct}} of their 1980s level or less at their low point, against {{dep_sq_forage_low:pct}} without a programme. From the cod-free state the catches are very large, about {{alt_F1_D10_yield:,.0f}} kt a year over a 10-year programme at F 1, but they come from a boom the model never produces from history. There is no sand lance fishery on this shelf, and herring stocks are managed separately; their current status was not compiled here.

What evidence would change the answer

  1. Direct measurements, during cod spawning on this shelf, of forage fish eating cod eggs and larvae. The conditional result rests on them causing several percent of young-cod deaths; North Sea estimates are far lower.
  2. A link between forage-fish abundance and young-cod survival or recruitment in the survey record. The direct test so far found none.
  3. Better estimates of adult cod mortality and its causes, including grey seals. If adult deaths stay high, the forage lever does not matter in the model.
  4. A model that reproduces the observed forage boom ({{survey_forage_1995_2004:x}}) and its later decline ({{survey_forage_2005_12:x}}). Until then, the role reversal at the size of boom that actually happened is untested.
  5. The present state of herring and other forage stocks on the shelf.

Limits of the model

  • It cannot reproduce the forage boom: {{hindA_forage_ratio:x}} with fishing only and {{hindB_forage_ratio:x}} with higher adult cod deaths, against {{survey_forage_1995_2004:x}} in the survey, or the later decline.
  • Haddock, silver hake and plaice stay between {{hindA_other_min:x}} and {{hindA_other_max:x}} in the fishing-only run, where the survey shows {{survey_plaice_1995_2004:x}} to {{survey_haddock_1995_2004:x}}.
  • One assumption, s = {{s_value}}, has no data behind it.
  • The modelled community is fragile. Cod collapses under steady cod fishing at F {{cod_collapse_F_model}}, below the {{cod_collapse_F_target}} expected, and recovered cod overshoot and push out the other groundfish.
  • Seals, temperature and changes in cod growth and maturity are not modelled. Recovery times move by up to {{numcheck_max_shift_yr}} years with numerical settings.
About

About the models

Three versions of a multi-species size-spectrum model, built with the R package mizer (version {{mizer_version_text}}), were tried. The first could not test the idea: forage fish caused almost none of the deaths of young cod, and cod recovered within a few years of 1994. The second made deaths of eggs and larvae explicit and tied plankton to Scotian Shelf data; it reproduced the forage boom only by forcing cod and other groundfish down with fishing far above documented levels. The third, used on this page, adds the explicit assumption s = {{s_value}}, recalibrates the 1980s state with it, and uses documented fishing only. None of the three finds that the role reversal by itself explains the failed recovery.
